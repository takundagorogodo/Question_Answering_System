TITLE: Semantic Analysis
PURPOSE: Retrieval-friendly NLP knowledge for a generative question-answering system.
USAGE: Each statement is written as an atomic knowledge unit so semantic retrieval can return focused evidence.

SECTION: Core concepts
Semantic analysis determines aspects of meaning expressed by language.
Lexical semantics studies the meanings and relationships of words and lexical units.
Compositional semantics studies how meanings combine to form larger meanings.
Sentence semantics concerns the meaning of complete sentences.
Discourse semantics studies meaning across multiple sentences and discourse structures.
Semantic representation converts meaning into a structured or vector-based form.
Semantic similarity measures how closely two texts or concepts are related in meaning.
Semantic relatedness is broader than similarity and can include associations between concepts.
Semantic interpretation often uses syntax, context, lexical knowledge, and world knowledge.
Semantic analysis supports question answering, search, translation, summarization, and information extraction.

SECTION: Lexical semantics
Synonymy describes words or expressions with similar meanings.
Antonymy describes contrasting meanings.
Hyponymy describes a specific-to-general semantic relationship.
Hypernymy describes a general-to-specific hierarchy from the perspective of the broader concept.
Meronymy describes a part-to-whole relationship.
Polysemy describes multiple related senses of a lexical item.
Homonymy describes identical forms with unrelated meanings.
Collocation describes words that commonly occur together.
Semantic fields group words associated with a related conceptual domain.
Lexical semantic resources can encode these relationships for NLP systems.

SECTION: Compositional semantics
Compositionality states that the meaning of a larger expression depends on its parts and how they are combined.
Predicate-argument structure represents relationships between predicates and their arguments.
Semantic roles describe functions such as agent, patient, instrument, and location.
Quantifiers express concepts such as every, some, and no.
Negation changes the truth conditions or polarity of an expression.
Modality expresses possibility, necessity, obligation, or related meanings.
Scope describes which part of an expression is affected by an operator such as negation or a quantifier.
Semantic parsing maps natural language to a structured meaning representation.
Logical forms can represent relations and conditions explicitly.
Compositional interpretation can require both syntactic structure and lexical meaning.

SECTION: Semantic representations
Knowledge graphs represent entities and relationships as structured graphs.
Predicate logic can represent entities, relations, quantifiers, and conditions.
Frame semantics represents meaning using frames with associated roles.
Semantic role labeling assigns semantic roles to arguments of predicates.
Distributional representations encode meaning using patterns of word usage.
Word embeddings represent lexical items as vectors.
Contextual embeddings represent tokens according to their surrounding text.
Sentence embeddings represent larger spans as vectors suitable for semantic comparison.
Vector similarity can support semantic search and retrieval.
Symbolic and neural semantic representations can be combined in hybrid systems.

SECTION: Semantic similarity and entailment
Semantic similarity asks how close two expressions are in meaning.
Semantic textual similarity compares the meanings of two text segments.
Natural language inference determines whether one statement entails, contradicts, or is neutral with respect to another.
Entailment means that a hypothesis follows from the information expressed by a premise under the task definition.
Contradiction means that the hypothesis conflicts with the premise.
Neutrality means that the premise does not establish either entailment or contradiction.
Similarity can be lexical, syntactic, semantic, or contextual.
Embedding-based similarity uses vector representations to compare texts.
Cross-encoder models can jointly encode two texts for more detailed comparison.
Semantic matching is important for retrieval and duplicate-question detection.

SECTION: Discourse and context
Discourse context can change the interpretation of an individual sentence.
Coreference links references across sentences.
Discourse relations describe connections such as cause, contrast, elaboration, and sequence.
Topic context can help interpret ambiguous terms.
Temporal context helps determine relationships between events.
Speaker information can affect interpretation in dialogue.
Pragmatics studies how context and communicative intentions influence meaning.
Deixis depends on contextual information such as speaker, time, or location.
Implicit meaning may not be explicitly stated in the words themselves.
Context-aware semantic models can combine sentence and document information.

SECTION: Semantic parsing
Semantic parsing converts natural language into a structured semantic representation.
Task-oriented semantic parsing may map questions to database queries.
Abstract meaning representations can encode relations independently of surface wording.
Frame-based parsing identifies a semantic frame and its roles.
Logical-form parsing can support reasoning over structured knowledge.
Sequence-to-sequence models can generate structured semantic representations.
Constrained decoding can ensure that generated semantic forms follow a required syntax.
Semantic parsers can be evaluated by exact match or execution accuracy when outputs are executable.
Ambiguous language can produce multiple plausible semantic parses.
Domain-specific semantic parsers often use an application-specific ontology.

SECTION: Applications and limitations
Semantic search retrieves content based on meaning rather than only exact word overlap.
Question answering uses semantic interpretation to map questions to relevant evidence.
Information extraction uses semantic relations to structure facts from text.
Machine translation requires semantic interpretation to preserve meaning across languages.
Text summarization benefits from semantic representations that distinguish central information.
Chatbots use semantic analysis to identify user intent and relevant entities.
Semantic analysis can fail when context is missing or world knowledge is required.
Distributional representations may capture associations without explicitly representing logical truth.
Neural semantic models can produce plausible but unsupported interpretations.
High-quality semantic systems should combine context, task constraints, and appropriate evaluation.
