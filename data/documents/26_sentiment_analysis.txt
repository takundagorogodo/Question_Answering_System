TITLE: Sentiment Analysis
PURPOSE: Retrieval-friendly knowledge for a generative question-answering system.

SECTION: Core definitions
Sentiment analysis is the computational identification of opinions, attitudes, or emotional polarity expressed in text.
Opinion mining is a closely related term that focuses on extracting and analyzing opinions from textual data.
Polarity usually represents whether a statement is positive, negative, or neutral.
Binary sentiment classification assigns text to two classes such as positive and negative.
Three-way sentiment classification commonly uses positive, negative, and neutral classes.
Fine-grained sentiment may use labels such as very negative, negative, neutral, positive, and very positive.
Emotion classification differs from sentiment polarity because it can distinguish emotions such as joy, anger, fear, sadness, and surprise.
Subjectivity detection determines whether a text expresses a personal opinion or mainly states objective information.
Sentiment analysis can operate at document, sentence, aspect, or entity level.
Sentiment analysis is commonly treated as a natural language processing task.

SECTION: Levels and granularity
Document-level sentiment assigns one overall sentiment to an entire document.
Sentence-level sentiment assigns sentiment to an individual sentence.
Aspect-based sentiment analysis identifies sentiment toward specific aspects of an entity.
Entity-level sentiment identifies the sentiment associated with a named entity such as a product or organization.
Aspect extraction finds the attributes or features that are being discussed.
Aspect polarity determines whether the opinion about a particular aspect is positive, negative, or neutral.
Opinion target extraction identifies the object or feature toward which an opinion is directed.
Opinion holder extraction identifies the person, group, or source expressing an opinion.
Opinion expression extraction identifies the words or phrases that convey the opinion.
Fine-grained analysis is useful when one sentence contains different opinions about different aspects.

SECTION: Text preprocessing
Tokenization divides text into tokens such as words, subwords, or punctuation symbols.
Normalization can reduce irrelevant variation such as inconsistent capitalization.
Lowercasing can help traditional models but may remove useful information such as emphasis or proper-name distinctions.
Stop-word removal can reduce feature dimensionality but may remove sentiment-bearing words in some tasks.
Negation handling is important because words such as not can reverse or alter sentiment.
Stemming reduces words to approximate roots by removing affixes.
Lemmatization maps inflected words to dictionary base forms.
Emoji processing can preserve useful sentiment signals that would be lost by deleting symbols.
Hashtags may contain sentiment-bearing words and should not automatically be removed.
URLs, usernames, and repeated punctuation may require task-specific normalization.

SECTION: Lexicon-based methods
A sentiment lexicon is a dictionary that associates words or phrases with sentiment values or categories.
Lexicon-based sentiment analysis can work without supervised training data.
Positive and negative word counts are simple lexicon-based sentiment features.
Valence scores can assign numerical strength to sentiment-bearing terms.
Negation can be handled by modifying the contribution of nearby sentiment words.
Intensifiers such as very can increase the strength of a sentiment expression.
Diminishers such as somewhat can reduce sentiment intensity.
Domain-specific lexicons are useful because a word can have different sentiment meanings in different domains.
Lexicon methods may struggle with sarcasm, context, and implicit sentiment.
Combining several lexicons can increase coverage but may also introduce conflicting word scores.

SECTION: Machine learning methods
Naive Bayes is a probabilistic classifier commonly used as a baseline for text classification.
Logistic regression is a strong linear baseline for sentiment classification with sparse text features.
Support vector machines can perform well on high-dimensional text representations.
Decision trees can model nonlinear rules but may be less competitive than linear models for sparse text.
Random forests combine multiple decision trees and can handle nonlinear feature interactions.
Neural networks can learn distributed representations for sentiment classification.
Recurrent neural networks model sequential information and were widely used before transformer models became dominant.
Convolutional neural networks can detect local n-gram-like patterns in text.
Transformers use attention mechanisms to model relationships among tokens.
Pretrained language models can be fine-tuned for sentiment classification using labeled examples.

SECTION: Features and representations
Bag-of-words represents text using counts or binary indicators of vocabulary items.
Term frequency measures how often a term occurs in a document.
TF-IDF reduces the influence of terms that occur in many documents.
Word embeddings represent words as dense numerical vectors.
Contextual embeddings represent a token using its surrounding context.
N-grams capture sequences of adjacent tokens.
Character n-grams can help with spelling variation and noisy user-generated text.
Sentence embeddings represent a complete sentence or passage as a vector.
Feature scaling may be useful for some machine learning algorithms.
Feature selection can reduce noise and computational cost.

SECTION: Context, negation, sarcasm
Negation scope is the portion of text affected by a negating expression.
A sentiment model that ignores negation can incorrectly classify phrases such as not good.
Double negation can produce meanings that differ from simple negative-word counting.
Sarcasm occurs when literal wording differs from the intended attitude or meaning.
Irony can make literal sentiment cues misleading.
Context from previous sentences may be necessary to determine sentiment.
Conversational sentiment may depend on speaker turns and dialogue history.
Domain context can change the sentiment meaning of a word.
Pragmatic cues such as punctuation, emojis, and discourse markers can affect interpretation.
Sarcasm detection is often treated as a separate but related classification task.

SECTION: Evaluation
Accuracy is the proportion of predictions that are correct.
Precision measures the proportion of predicted positives that are actually positive.
Recall measures the proportion of actual positives that are correctly identified.
F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.
Macro F1 averages class-level F1 scores and gives each class equal weight.
Micro F1 aggregates contributions across classes and is influenced by class frequency.
A confusion matrix shows counts of correct and incorrect predictions by class.
Class imbalance can make accuracy misleading.
Stratified splitting helps preserve class proportions across train and validation sets.
Test data should be kept separate from training and model selection when possible.

SECTION: Applications and limitations
Sentiment analysis is used for analyzing customer reviews.
Businesses can use sentiment analysis to summarize large collections of feedback.
Social media monitoring can use sentiment models to study reactions to products or events.
Support-ticket sentiment can help identify messages that contain dissatisfaction.
Market research can use sentiment analysis as one signal among many qualitative and quantitative measures.
Sentiment analysis can support survey and feedback analysis.
Automated sentiment predictions should not automatically be treated as ground truth.
Bias in training data can cause systematic differences in performance across domains or groups.
Multilingual sentiment analysis requires language-aware resources and evaluation.
Human review can be useful for ambiguous, sarcastic, or high-impact cases.
