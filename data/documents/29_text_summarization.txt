TITLE: Text Summarization
PURPOSE: Retrieval-friendly knowledge for a generative question-answering system.

SECTION: Core concepts
Text summarization creates a shorter representation of a source while preserving important information.
Abstractive summarization generates new wording that represents the source content.
Extractive summarization selects sentences or spans from the source.
Single-document summarization summarizes one source document.
Multi-document summarization combines information from multiple documents.
Query-focused summarization emphasizes information relevant to a specified question or topic.
Generic summarization aims to capture the main content without a specific query.
Headline generation produces very short summaries suitable for titles or headlines.
Meeting summarization condenses dialogue, decisions, and action items.
Long-document summarization requires handling more content than a standard model context may support.

SECTION: Summarization pipeline
Preprocessing prepares the source text for summarization.
Segmentation divides a document into sentences or other units.
Content selection identifies information that should be retained.
Content planning determines an ordering or structure for selected information.
Surface realization generates the final natural-language summary.
Length control specifies a target summary size.
Compression removes redundancy while retaining important facts.
Deduplication reduces repeated information in multi-document summaries.
Attribution can identify which source supports a summary claim.
Post-processing can enforce formatting and length constraints.

SECTION: Extractive methods
Extractive summarization can rank sentences by estimated importance.
Sentence frequency features can identify sentences containing salient terms.
TF-IDF can provide lexical importance features for sentence ranking.
Graph-based methods can represent sentences as nodes and similarity as edges.
Centrality measures can identify sentences that represent central document content.
Position features can be useful in some genres such as news articles.
Redundancy penalties can prevent selecting multiple highly similar sentences.
Maximum marginal relevance balances relevance against redundancy.
Supervised extractive models can learn sentence selection from labeled summaries.
Extractive systems preserve source wording but may produce less fluent summaries.

SECTION: Abstractive methods
Abstractive systems generate summary text rather than only copying source sentences.
Sequence-to-sequence models have been used extensively for abstractive summarization.
Attention helps a decoder focus on relevant source representations.
Transformer models are widely used for modern summarization.
Copy mechanisms can allow models to reproduce important source terms.
Pointer-style mechanisms can help preserve names and rare terminology.
Pretrained language models can be adapted to summarization tasks.
Instruction-tuned generators can summarize text according to explicit formatting requirements.
Abstractive models can paraphrase source information.
Abstractive generation creates a risk of factual errors or unsupported details.

SECTION: Quality and evaluation
ROUGE measures overlap between generated and reference summaries using lexical units.
ROUGE-1 evaluates unigram overlap.
ROUGE-2 evaluates bigram overlap.
ROUGE-L uses a longest-common-subsequence-based comparison.
ROUGE is useful for benchmarking but does not fully measure factuality.
BERT-style semantic metrics compare contextual representations between summaries.
Human evaluation can assess relevance, coherence, readability, and factual consistency.
Faithfulness asks whether the summary is supported by the source.
Coverage asks whether important source information is represented.
Conciseness asks whether the summary avoids unnecessary detail.

SECTION: Factuality and hallucination
Summary hallucination occurs when a summary introduces information not supported by the source.
Entity errors can change names, organizations, locations, or other key facts.
Numerical errors can alter quantities, dates, percentages, or measurements.
Negation errors can reverse the meaning of a source statement.
Cross-document synthesis can accidentally merge facts from different sources.
Attribution errors can associate a claim with the wrong source.
Fact verification can compare generated claims against the source.
Sentence-level source alignment can help identify unsupported summary statements.
Retrieval-based generation can improve access to source evidence but does not guarantee factuality.
High-stakes summaries may require human verification.

SECTION: Long and multi-document summarization
Long-document summarization may require hierarchical processing.
Hierarchical summarization can summarize sections before producing a document-level summary.
Sliding-window processing divides long documents into overlapping segments.
Map-reduce summarization summarizes chunks and then combines their summaries.
Multi-document summarization should identify information shared across sources.
Contradictory sources require explicit handling rather than silent merging.
Source timestamps can matter when summarizing evolving events or records.
Redundancy is a major challenge in multi-document summarization.
Clustering can group related documents or passages before summarization.
Query-focused retrieval can reduce the amount of irrelevant material presented to a summarizer.

SECTION: Practical controls
Maximum output length can constrain summary size.
Bullet summaries can improve scanability for structured information.
Paragraph summaries can preserve more narrative flow.
Audience level can determine how much terminology should be explained.
Style instructions can request formal, simple, technical, or executive summaries.
Key-point extraction can provide a compact alternative to prose summarization.
Action-item extraction focuses on tasks, owners, and deadlines.
Decision extraction identifies conclusions reached in meetings or documents.
Risk extraction identifies stated risks, limitations, or uncertainties.
Summary prompts should clearly identify the source material and desired output format.

SECTION: Applications and limitations
Summarization can support research by reducing the reading burden of long documents.
News summarization can provide concise representations of articles.
Meeting summarization can capture decisions and follow-up actions.
Customer feedback summarization can aggregate recurring themes.
Legal and compliance summaries can help organize documents but may require human review.
Educational summarization can create revision notes from source material.
Summaries can omit details that matter for a particular user.
A concise summary is not necessarily a complete summary.
Model-generated summaries may reflect biases or omissions in the source and model.
Summary quality should be evaluated against the intended use case.
