Hallucination in Generative Language Models

In the context of natural language processing, hallucination refers to a situation where a generative language model produces text that is fluent, confident, and grammatically correct, but factually incorrect or entirely unsupported by any real source of information. The term is borrowed loosely from human psychology, since the model is, in a sense, generating a plausible-sounding statement that has no basis in reality, without any internal signal that it is doing so.

Hallucination happens because generative language models are fundamentally trained to predict the next most likely word given the preceding text, based on patterns learned from enormous amounts of training data. When a model is asked something it genuinely does not know, it does not have a built-in mechanism to simply say "I don't know" unless it has been specifically trained or prompted to behave that way. Instead, it tends to produce the most statistically plausible continuation, which can sound entirely convincing while being wrong.

Hallucination is a serious practical problem for any question answering system, because a fluent but incorrect answer can be more dangerous than an obviously broken one: a user has no easy way to tell that the answer is wrong just by reading it. This is especially risky in domains where incorrect information could lead to poor decisions.

Several strategies can help reduce, though not completely eliminate, hallucination. Retrieval-augmented generation is one of the most effective, because it grounds the model's answer in real, retrieved source text rather than relying purely on the model's internal memory. Explicit prompt instructions, such as telling the model to answer only using the provided context and to say that information is unavailable if the answer cannot be found there, further reduce the tendency to invent information. A retrieval relevance threshold can also help: if no retrieved passage is similar enough to the question, the system can decline to answer rather than forcing a generation from weak or irrelevant context. Some systems also display a confidence or relevance score alongside each answer, giving the user a signal of how strongly the answer is supported by the retrieved material.

Measuring hallucination rate is an important part of rigorously evaluating a generative question answering system. This is typically done by testing the system with questions that are deliberately outside its knowledge base, and checking whether it correctly declines to answer rather than inventing a plausible-sounding but false response.
