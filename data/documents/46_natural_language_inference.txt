TITLE: NATURAL LANGUAGE INFERENCE
CATEGORY: NATURAL LANGUAGE PROCESSING
PURPOSE: Knowledge base for textual entailment, semantic reasoning, contradiction detection, and retrieval-augmented question answering.

SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION
001. Natural Language Inference is commonly abbreviated as NLI.
002. NLI determines the logical relationship between two pieces of natural language text.
003. The first text is usually called the premise.
004. The second text is usually called the hypothesis.
005. The premise provides information against which the hypothesis is evaluated.
006. The hypothesis is a statement whose relationship to the premise must be classified.
007. Standard NLI systems commonly predict entailment, contradiction, or neutral.
008. Entailment means that the premise supports the hypothesis as necessarily true under the task's interpretation.
009. Contradiction means that the premise conflicts with the hypothesis.
010. Neutral means that the premise does not establish either entailment or contradiction.
011. NLI is used in semantic understanding and textual reasoning.
012. It helps systems compare the meanings of sentences.
013. NLI can detect whether one statement supports another.
014. NLI can help identify conflicting statements in documents.
015. NLI is useful in some fact verification pipelines.
016. NLI models are commonly trained using labeled sentence pairs.
017. Transformer encoders are widely used for modern NLI classification.
018. NLI predictions are model judgments rather than guaranteed logical proofs.
019. Context and background assumptions can affect interpretation.
020. NLI should be used with awareness of model limitations.

SECTION 2: PREMISE AND HYPOTHESIS
021. The premise is the source statement used for inference.
022. The hypothesis is the statement evaluated against the premise.
023. Both texts may contain one sentence or multiple sentences.
024. A premise can describe an event, fact, action, or situation.
025. A hypothesis can restate the premise using different words.
026. A hypothesis can introduce a new detail absent from the premise.
027. A hypothesis can directly conflict with a statement in the premise.
028. The premise and hypothesis may use different grammatical structures.
029. Semantic similarity does not automatically establish entailment.
030. Two sentences can discuss the same subject but have different logical relationships.
031. A premise should be interpreted according to its stated content.
032. A hypothesis should not receive support from facts absent from the premise unless the task permits external knowledge.
033. Evaluation datasets generally define which reasoning assumptions are acceptable.
034. Negation can change the relationship between premise and hypothesis.
035. Quantifiers can affect whether a hypothesis follows from a premise.
036. Temporal expressions can affect inference.
037. Numerical statements require careful comparison.
038. Pronouns may need resolution before the relationship is clear.
039. Ambiguous text can make NLI classification difficult.
040. Correct NLI analysis depends on understanding both statements.

SECTION 3: ENTAILMENT
041. Entailment occurs when the premise supports the hypothesis as necessarily true under the relevant interpretation.
042. An entailed hypothesis does not introduce an unsupported factual requirement.
043. Premise: A dog is sleeping. Hypothesis: An animal is sleeping.
044. The example in line 43 represents entailment because a dog is an animal.
045. Premise: Maria bought three apples. Hypothesis: Maria bought fruit.
046. The example in line 45 represents entailment under the ordinary meaning of apples.
047. Premise: Every student passed the exam. Hypothesis: At least one student passed the exam.
048. The example in line 47 assumes that at least one student exists.
049. Entailment can involve synonymy.
050. Entailment can involve category relationships.
051. Entailment can involve paraphrasing.
052. Entailment can involve numerical reasoning.
053. Entailment can involve resolving references between sentences.
054. Entailment may require combining multiple facts in the premise.
055. The premise may contain more information than the hypothesis.
056. A hypothesis can be shorter than the premise and still be entailed.
057. An entailed hypothesis must be supported by the premise rather than merely being plausible.
058. Entailment is stronger than topical similarity.
059. In factual question answering, entailment can help assess evidence support.
060. NLI predictions of entailment should be checked for important high-stakes claims.

SECTION 4: CONTRADICTION
061. Contradiction occurs when the premise and hypothesis cannot both be true under the same interpretation.
062. Contradictory statements make incompatible claims.
063. Premise: The door is open. Hypothesis: The door is closed.
064. The example in line 63 represents a likely contradiction in an ordinary binary door-state context.
065. Premise: No students attended the class. Hypothesis: Some students attended the class.
066. The example in line 65 represents contradiction under the ordinary meaning of the statements.
067. Negation is a common source of contradiction.
068. Opposing quantities can create contradiction.
069. Conflicting dates can create contradiction.
070. Conflicting identities can create contradiction.
071. Contradiction can occur when one sentence denies an event asserted by another.
072. Different wording does not prevent two statements from contradicting each other.
073. A contradiction requires more than a difference in topic or emphasis.
074. Statements can differ without being contradictory.
075. A model should not label every unsupported hypothesis as a contradiction.
076. Contradiction detection is useful for reviewing inconsistent documents.
077. Contradiction detection can help compare answers with source evidence.
078. Temporal context can determine whether two apparently conflicting statements are compatible.
079. Qualifications and exceptions can change the interpretation of a contradiction.
080. NLI-based contradiction detection should account for context where available.

SECTION 5: NEUTRAL RELATIONSHIPS
081. Neutral means the premise neither entails nor contradicts the hypothesis.
082. A neutral hypothesis may be true, but the premise does not establish it.
083. Premise: A woman is reading a book. Hypothesis: The book is a mystery novel.
084. The example in line 83 is neutral because the genre is unspecified.
085. Premise: Ravi owns a bicycle. Hypothesis: Ravi owns a car.
086. The example in line 85 is neutral because car ownership is not stated.
087. Neutrality does not mean that the hypothesis is false.
088. Neutrality does not necessarily mean that the hypothesis is unrelated to the premise.
089. A hypothesis can be plausible and still be neutral.
090. Background knowledge should be handled according to the NLI task definition.
091. Missing information often leads to neutral classification.
092. A model should distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of contradiction.
093. Neutral examples help train models to avoid unsupported conclusions.
094. Neutrality is important for reliable question answering.
095. A question-answering system should not treat every related passage as proof.
096. Neutrality can indicate that additional evidence is required.
097. Some hypotheses require details not contained in the premise.
098. Ambiguous premises may prevent a confident relationship judgment.
099. Neutral classification depends on the assumptions used by the dataset.
100. Neutral predictions should be interpreted with the model's confidence and limitations.

SECTION 13: SEMANTIC SIMILARITY VERSUS ENTAILMENT
241. Semantic similarity measures how closely two texts resemble each other in meaning or representation.
242. Entailment evaluates whether one text supports the truth of another.
243. Similar sentences can have a contradiction relationship.
244. Premise: The light is on. Hypothesis: The light is off. The sentences are related but contradictory.
245. A hypothesis can be entailed without sharing many exact words with the premise.
246. Premise: A physician examined the patient. Hypothesis: A doctor checked the patient. The relationship is likely entailment.
247. Embedding similarity alone cannot reliably distinguish every contradiction.
248. Negation may preserve topic similarity while reversing meaning.
249. NLI requires directional reasoning from premise to hypothesis.
250. Semantic similarity is often treated as a symmetric comparison.
251. Entailment is generally directional.
252. If a premise entails a hypothesis, the reverse relationship may not hold.
253. A dog is an animal, but the word animal does not entail dog.
254. A paraphrase often supports entailment in both directions.
255. Not all related statements are paraphrases.
256. Similarity search can retrieve candidate evidence for NLI analysis.

258. Combining retrieval and inference can improve some question-answering workflows.
259. The systems must be evaluated for the errors introduced by each stage.
