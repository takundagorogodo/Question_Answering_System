Tokenization

Tokenization is the process of breaking a piece of text into smaller units called tokens, which a computer program can then process one at a time. It is usually the very first step in any natural language processing pipeline, because raw text arrives as an unbroken string of characters, and a model needs some way to divide that string into meaningful pieces before it can do anything useful with it.

The simplest form of tokenization splits text on whitespace and punctuation, treating each word as a token. For example, the sentence "NLP is fascinating!" would be split into the tokens "NLP", "is", "fascinating", and "!". This word-level approach is easy to understand, but it runs into problems with languages that do not use spaces between words, with made-up or rare words, and with the sheer size of the vocabulary it produces, since every distinct word form needs its own entry.

Modern transformer-based models typically use a more sophisticated approach called subword tokenization. Instead of treating whole words as the smallest unit, these methods break rare or complex words into smaller, more frequent pieces. For example, the word "tokenization" itself might be split into pieces like "token" and "ization". Common words remain a single token, while rare or unfamiliar words are represented as a sequence of smaller, familiar subword pieces. This keeps the vocabulary size manageable while still allowing the model to represent virtually any word, including ones it has never seen before, by combining known subword pieces.

Popular subword tokenization algorithms include Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), WordPiece, and SentencePiece. Each works slightly differently, but they share the same core idea: build a vocabulary of subword units by starting from individual characters and progressively merging the most frequently occurring pairs, until a target vocabulary size is reached.

Tokenization matters for a generative question answering system in several practical ways. First, the length limit of a language model is usually measured in tokens, not words or characters, so understanding tokenization helps explain why very long documents or contexts sometimes get cut off. Second, the way a question is tokenized can affect how well the model understands it, particularly for unusual technical terms, misspellings, or code snippets. Finally, when we talk about "chunking" a document for retrieval, the chunk size is also typically measured in tokens rather than raw characters, which is why tokenization is one of the first concepts to understand before working with chunking or embeddings.
